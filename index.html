01 / About the author

About the Author

Maria Agnes Prieto

Maria Agnes Prieto is a Filipino essayist known for “Earth Day,” a reflective essay written to commemorate the global Earth Day celebration of 1990. Detailed public biographical records about her are scarce; what can be established comes largely from the essay itself and its historical context.

Evidence from the text · Writer’s voice

The essay is anchored in the events of 1990: US President George Bush’s resistance to a carbon-dioxide treaty, Jose Lutzenberger’s appointment as Brazil’s Environment Secretary, and the Texas coastal cleanup — placing it at the first modern Earth Day (April 1990).

Prieto writes as an engaged Filipino citizen, citing local events (the children’s suit against DENR Secretary Jun Factoran, the Calaca and Lemery picket) and Filipino folklore (Mariang Makiling, Mariang Dolores, the anitos), blending global reporting with Philippine culture.

02 / Introduction

Introduction

“Earth Day” by Maria Agnes Prieto is a reflective essay written to mark Earth Day, a global event that “linked concerned citizens together in litter clean-ups, festivals, meditation sessions, tree-planting events, theater presentations, rallies as well as bikathons all over the world.”

The essay captures an “Earth Renaissance”: a worldwide awakening to environmental crisis, arguing that real change depends on individual commitment, not just governments and corporations.

03 / Summary

Summary

The global event

More than one hundred million people in 115 countries joined Earth Day, “touted to be the largest global demonstration in history”; even Mt. Everest was cleaned by fifteen mountaineer ecologists.

The crisis

Jolted by water shortages, pollution, floods, erratic weather, brownouts, famines, and nuclear risk, humanity realizes nature, “too long ignored and abused,” “now seems to be in its death throes.”

The turning point

The writer calls for “give and take” instead of a “take-take situation,” and stresses that concern is an “individual commitment.”

Ordinary heroes

Children sued DENR Secretary Jun Factoran; residents of Calaca and Lemery picketed a coal plant; Jose Lutzenberger became Brazil’s Environment Secretary; Linda Maraniss started the Texas coastal cleanup.

The way forward

A return to the reverence of the ancients and simple everyday acts — proper disposal, refusing aerosols, rechargeable batteries, homemade cleaners — closing with Hopkins: “nature is never spent.”

04 / Theme & main idea

From “take-take” to give and take

The central theme is individual responsibility for the environment: “Who says you can’t change the world?” — with the emphasis on the YOU, singular, “for though mass action is emphatic, it is still the individual concern and action that fuels the environmentalist movement.”

05 / Voice

Who is speaking?

I

A first-person narrator

No fictional characters: the voice is an unnamed narrator using “we,” “us,” and “you” — a fellow citizen of the earth, not a detached observer.

We

Roles the speaker takes

A witness reporting real events; a critic of consumerism and stalled governments; a persuader who directly addresses the reader.

You

The addressed audience

Every reader, through “you” — plus the voices cited as evidence: George Bush, Jun Factoran, Lutzenberger, Collor de Mello, Maraniss, the children who sued, the citizens of Calaca and Lemery.

06 / Moral lesson

Care becomes visible through habits

Lessons from the essay

  • Care begins in little things: “every effort counts… care is always manifested in little things.”
  • Nature gives, humans must give back: “The earth will give back that caring to you.”
  • Do not wait for others: institutional efforts “are slowly growing towards awareness but these efforts are insufficient.”
  • Reconnect with nature’s rhythms, as the ancients who “kept close to these rhythms” knew.

In practice

For students: dispose of “plastic and styrofoam junk” properly; join campus clean-up drives.

At home: choose “lotions, gel or non-aerosol sprays”; clean with “baking soda and white vinegar”; use rechargeable batteries.

As a citizen: speak up like the children who sued the DENR and the residents who picketed for cleaner air.

07 / Literary analysis I

Type, genre, and tone

Type · Classification

Type: nonfiction prose
Classification: reflective and persuasive essay

Genre

Reflective essay and social commentary on the environment, closing with a poetic quotation (Hopkins’ “God’s Grandeur”), blending journalism, argument, and poetry.

Tone

  • Urgent — “death throes”; “The earth cannot wait.”
  • Critical — “needs study, not immediate action”; “Where’s the funding?”
  • Hopeful and reverent — “nature is never spent.”

Characters

None fictional — a first-person narrator (“we,” “us,” “you”), a concerned Filipino citizen; real figures (Bush, Factoran, Lutzenberger, Maraniss, the children’s suit, Calaca and Lemery) appear as evidence.

08 / Literary analysis II

Setting, conflict, and plot

Setting

Place: worldwide (115 countries, Mt. Everest); the Philippines (DENR, Calaca, Lemery, Laguna, Banahaw); the Texas Gulf coast; Brazil. Time: Earth Day, 1990. Social setting: a global citizens’ movement confronting consumerism and institutional inaction.

Conflict

  • Man vs. Society: citizens vs. polluting industries and stalled governments.
  • Man vs. Himself: greed and apathy of the “me” generation vs. the call to care.
  • Humanity vs. Nature: nature “in its death throes” after abuse.

Plot (flow of the argument)

  • Exposition: the global Earth Day event.
  • Complication: the inventory of environmental damage.
  • Crisis: “It’s got to be give and take now.”
  • Climax: proof that individuals win — Lutzenberger, Maraniss, the children’s suit.
  • Resolution: everyday actions plus Hopkins’ hopeful vision.

09 / Literary analysis III

Point of view, theme, and style

Point of view · Theme

First person (“we,” “us”) shifting to direct second-person address (“you”) — the speaker argues side by side with the reader, then turns the challenge onto them.

Theme: individual responsibility; stewardship over exploitation (“give and take”); inner balance reflected in ecological balance.

Symbolism & style / structure

Symbols: Mt. Everest (even remote places are touched), the anitos and Mariang Makiling / Mariang Dolores (reverence for nature), Hopkins’ “shook foil” and “bright wings” (renewal).

Style / structure: essay moving from event to evidence to personal appeal; anecdote + argument + poetic allusion; rhetorical questions and parallelism; ends by quoting a poem.

10 / Literary analysis IV

Imagery and language

Imagery and symbolism

Decay imagery: “The forests are shorn of their trees,” seas as “dumping grounds,” shores “littered by Coke cans and plastic junk.” Living imagery: “the rivers were gentle sisters, the winds, an emphatic brother.” Symbols: Mt. Everest, the anitos and Mariang Makiling, “the ooze of oil crushed” (renewal from darkness).

Language and devices

  • Personification: “Nature… has began to falter”; “gentle sisters” rivers.
  • Antithesis / parallelism: “take-take” vs. “give and take.”
  • Direct address: “It is up to you and others like you…”
  • Rhetorical question: “Who says you can’t change the world?”
  • Allusion: the closing quotation of Hopkins’ “God’s Grandeur” (1877).

11 / Conclusion

The Earth cannot wait

“Earth Day” documents the largest global demonstration in history — and turns it into a personal challenge, from “take-take” to “give and take,” from mass movement to the individual “YOU.”

Decades later, the same warnings remain in our headlines. Prieto’s argument that individuals, not institutions, drive change is more relevant than ever.

“Take one small action for the earth this week — and let care grow from there.”Closing message prepared for this presentation
